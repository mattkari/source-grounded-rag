This answer will be printed as one section of an article for general readers, drafted from more
than one source. The rules of the system prompt all still hold. These rules of form are added:

1. Write plain, connected prose for a reader who has not read the sources. Say what each source
   argues in your own words. Do not write about "the evidence", "the passages" or "the collection".
2. Put each `[En]` marker at the END of the clause or sentence it supports, before the full stop.
   A marker is never a word in the sentence. Never write "[E5] states", "[E4] notes that",
   "according to [E2]" or "as [E3] shows". Name the author instead, as the passage header gives
   the name: "Karimov describes three faculties [E5]."
   One marker per bracket: `[E1] [E2]`, never `[E1, E2]`, `[E1; E2]` or `[E1-E3]`. A joined
   bracket cannot be resolved to a page, and the section is rejected.
3. Each sentence reports one source. Name that source's author in the sentence, and give the
   sentence only the markers of that source's passages. When two sources bear on the same point,
   write two sentences, one for each. A reader checks every sentence against one page of one
   source, and a sentence that mixes two cannot be checked.
4. Do not write that scholars agree, that the literature shows, or that something is generally
   held. Say which named author says it. If two authors say the same thing, say so of those two.
5. Paraphrase. A direct quotation is at most 25 words, and quotations together are at most one
   fifth of the section. Longer material is paraphrased and carries its marker.
6. Do not use em dashes.
7. `limitations`: at most two items. Write each as one short sentence a reader can follow,
   beginning "The sources do not", for example "The sources do not discuss how the two terms
   are related [E2]."
8. `ai_interpretation`: at most one item, and only if it adds something a reader needs. Say who
   acts exactly as the passage does: do not turn what an author describes into something that
   author did or decided.
